# Does Removing the Only Bath Affect Your Home's Resale Appeal?

Removing your home's only bath may change its appeal to buyers, but it does not automatically reduce its value. A buyer who wants a bath may see its absence as a drawback. Someone who prioritises a spacious shower may prefer the new layout. The decision depends on your property, its likely buyers and what the renovation improves.

Before planning bathroom renovations on the Sunshine Coast, separate two questions: will the change make your bathroom better to use, and will it suit the people most likely to buy your home?

## TL;DR

- Buyer appeal and sale price are different. A feature can matter to some buyers without having a predictable dollar value.
- Removing the only bath deserves more consideration than removing a second bath.
- Assess the whole bathroom. Layout, condition, storage and finish matter alongside the fixtures.
- Speak with local agents before renovating specifically to sell. Ask for evidence from comparable properties.
- Compare alternatives before committing. A different bath, vanity or layout may preserve both bathing options.
- Avoid spending on assumptions. A new bathroom does not guarantee you will recover the full renovation cost.

## Buyer appeal is different from property value

Buyer appeal describes how well your home suits someone's preferences and needs. Property value requires a broader assessment of the home and market.

A buyer might dislike a shower-only bathroom but still purchase the property because its location, size and overall condition suit them. Another might prefer the bathroom but decide against the home for unrelated reasons.

That is why a statement such as "removing a bath reduces your sale price by 10%" needs strong, relevant evidence. The sources supporting this article do not establish a universal percentage or dollar penalty.

[The Real Estate Institute of Queensland advises homeowners that improvements do not offer a guaranteed path to added value and recommends seeking advice specific to the property before spending.][1]

The useful question is whether removing the bath solves a meaningful problem without creating a bigger objection for your likely buyers.

## Consider who might buy your home

Start with the property itself. A family house, compact apartment and retirement-focused home may attract different buyers, but avoid assuming that everyone in a category wants the same bathroom.

The following are buyer scenarios to investigate, rather than predictions about every purchaser.

### Buyers who want a family bathroom

A household that regularly bathes young children may value having a tub. Removing the only one could mean they need to consider another renovation after purchasing.

If your home is likely to attract these buyers, ask local agents whether the absence of a bath has been a recurring objection at comparable properties.

### Buyers who prioritise shower space

Other buyers may place more importance on a generous walk-in shower, useful storage and room to move.

That does not make every shower-only layout attractive. A narrow entrance or awkward screen position may undermine the benefit of removing the bath.

### Buyers thinking about future access

Some buyers may be considering how a bathroom will work as their needs change.

[Your Home, the Australian Government's housing design guide, identifies circulation space, slip-resistant surfaces, showers without hobs and provision for future grab rails as adaptable design considerations.][2] These features need to be assessed together; removing a bath alone does not create an accessible bathroom.

## Assess the whole bathroom

Do not let the bath become the only feature you evaluate.

A room can retain a bath and still be uncomfortable to use. A shower-only bathroom can look spacious but lack storage or practical fittings.

Review these elements before deciding:

| Element | Consideration |
|---------|---|
| **Layout** | Can people move between fixtures and open doors comfortably? |
| **Vanity** | Does the bathroom vanity provide usable drawers and bench space? |
| **Storage** | Is there enough bathroom storage for toiletries, cleaning products and towels? |
| **Lighting** | Does the bathroom lighting suit the mirror and everyday tasks? |
| **Finishes** | Are the floor and wall tiles in good condition and consistent with the room? |
| **Fittings** | Is the tapware practical, working properly and positioned conveniently? |

[Reece's renovation planning guidance considers the users, bathing preferences, vanity placement and storage together.][3] That is a useful approach when comparing complete layouts.

Condition also deserves attention. [REIQ's advice for preparing a property for sale highlights addressing maintenance issues in bathrooms, rather than assuming a major renovation is always necessary.][4]

## Keeping the bath versus removing it

| Situation | Reason to consider keeping it | Reason to explore removing it |
|-----------|-------------------------------|------------------------------|
| **Your household uses the bath** | It serves a practical purpose now. | Removal would need to deliver a more important benefit. |
| **The bathroom is cramped** | A smaller bath or revised layout may help. | A shower-only layout may improve circulation and storage. |
| **You plan to sell soon** | Relevant local buyer feedback may support retaining it. | Agents may identify the existing layout as a greater drawback. |
| **You plan to stay for years** | Continued enjoyment may justify its footprint. | Daily shower comfort may matter more to your household. |
| **Another bathroom has a bath** | Retaining this one gives additional choice. | Removing a second bath preserves a bathing option elsewhere. |
| **Access is becoming difficult** | An individual assessment may identify suitable changes. | A carefully designed shower may better suit the user. |

These considerations help structure the decision. They are not a valuation formula.

## Explore alternatives before removing the only bath

You may have more than two options.

### Choose a different bath

The existing tub may be the wrong size or shape for the room. Compare its footprint with other models and check the internal bathing dimensions, not just the overall length.

Our guide to [Freestanding vs Built-In Baths: Space, Cleaning and Practicality](#recommended-reading) explores space, cleaning and practical differences.

### Reconsider the vanity and door arrangement

A different vanity depth or position may improve movement without removing the bath. Check drawer openings and door swings on a measured plan before ordering products.

Good bathroom layout planning should compare the available options rather than assume the current fixture positions are the only workable arrangement.

### Check whether both fixtures can fit comfortably

Having a bath and shower is only useful if the completed room functions well. Avoid squeezing in both at the expense of practical access.

See [Can You Fit a Bath and Separate Shower in a Small Bathroom?](#recommended-reading) for the considerations to work through.

### Consider a shower over the bath

This can retain both functions within one footprint, but stepping over the bath edge may be unsuitable for some users. Assess comfort, access and screen placement before treating it as the solution.

## Renovating to sell or renovating to stay

### If you are preparing to sell

Get property-specific advice before committing to demolition.

[REIQ recommends speaking with an agent early so you can identify where preparation spending is worthwhile.][5] Its selling guidance also recommends keeping relevant renovation documentation available for prospective buyers.

Compare three possible scopes:

1. Address maintenance and presentation.
2. Renovate while retaining a bath.
3. Renovate with a shower-only layout.

Ask what problem each option solves and whether the likely benefit justifies the cost. A higher anticipated sale price is not the same as a profit after renovation expenses.

### If you intend to stay

Give your own needs appropriate weight. Keeping an unused bath for years solely because an unknown future buyer might want it may not suit your household.

Equally, do not remove a bath you enjoy because a shower-only layout appears more fashionable.

Compare daily use, future needs and the cost of the work. Resale is one consideration within that decision.

### In either case, plan the work beneath the finishes

A new appearance should be supported by a clear renovation scope.

Discuss bathroom plumbing and drainage changes, surface preparation and how affected areas will be waterproofed.

[QBCC guidance stresses that bathroom waterproofing needs proper preparation, detailing and curing time.][6] It also highlights the importance of documenting the work and responsibilities.

Ask about the proposed shower screen and any changes to ventilation or electrical fittings so these are included in the scope.

## Questions to ask before committing

### Ask a local real estate agent:

- Who is most likely to buy this particular property?
- Have buyers objected to shower-only bathrooms in comparable homes?
- Can you show recent examples with similar size, condition and bathroom arrangements?
- Would you prioritise the bath decision, maintenance or another improvement?
- What supports your opinion beyond personal preference?

### Ask your renovation builder:

- Can we compare measured layouts with and without a bath?
- What space would removing it actually free up?
- Which fixtures or drainage connections need to move?
- What tiling and waterproofing are included?
- How will unexpected damage or variations be handled?
- What documents will I receive when the work is complete?

Use the answers to prepare a clear bathroom renovation quote, with inclusions and allowances you can compare.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does removing the only bath always reduce resale value?

No automatic reduction can be assumed. It may affect appeal for buyers who want a bath, but the outcome depends on the property, market and completed layout. Seek advice specific to your home.

### Is a bath necessary in a family home?

It may be valuable to a household that regularly uses one. However, buyer needs vary. If you are renovating to sell, investigate whether a bath is an important feature for the likely buyers of your property.

### Is removing a second bath a different decision?

Yes. If a suitable bath remains elsewhere, the home still offers that option. [Reece's guidance specifically suggests considering whether another bathroom already provides a bath when allocating space.][3]

### Will a larger shower make up for losing the bath?

It may suit some buyers better, but there is no guaranteed trade-off. Compare the actual shower dimensions, entrance, storage and overall layout rather than assuming bigger is always better.

### Should I renovate the bathroom before selling?

Not necessarily. Start with its condition, local agent feedback and a written scope. Maintenance or presentation work may be sufficient, while other bathrooms may justify a more substantial renovation.
