# Should You Keep the Bath or Replace It With a Shower?

Keep the bath if your household regularly uses it and the bathroom has enough space to function comfortably. Consider replacing it with a shower if it rarely gets used, makes the room cramped or is becoming difficult to step into. The right choice depends on your daily routine, available space and plans for the home.

For homeowners planning bathroom renovations on the Sunshine Coast, this decision should happen before choosing finishes. It shapes the layout, plumbing, storage and overall renovation scope.

## TL;DR

- Keep the bath when it earns its space, whether for children's bath time or your own regular use.
- Consider a bath-to-shower conversion when a larger shower would improve everyday life.
- Check the whole layout, including vanity drawers, door openings, storage and access.
- A walk-in shower is not automatically accessible. Entry, floor surfaces, fittings and circulation space all matter.
- Compare complete renovation scopes, including plumbing, waterproofing, tiling and finishing.
- If it is your only bath and you plan to sell soon, discuss the decision with local agents before removing it.

## Start with how you actually use the bathroom

Before deciding what looks best, ask what would make your mornings and evenings easier.

Does someone use the bath every week? Is it part of the children's bedtime routine? Or has it become an oversized shelf for washing baskets?

Then identify what frustrates you about the current room. You might need a wider shower entrance, better bathroom storage or enough space to open a drawer without stepping sideways.

Write down your three biggest priorities. Compare layouts against those priorities before choosing fixtures.

Thoughtful bathroom layout planning considers the entire room. [Reece's guidance recommends accounting for accurate dimensions, windows, doors and product placement together, rather than selecting individual items that may not work well together.][1]

## When keeping the bath makes sense

A bath deserves its place when someone genuinely enjoys or relies on using it.

Keeping one may suit your household if:

- Bath time is part of your family's regular routine.
- You enjoy soaking and would miss that option.
- The room comfortably accommodates both a bath and shower.
- You want to retain bathing options as household needs change.

Keeping a bath does not mean keeping the existing model or layout. A different shape, position or installation style may work better.

For example, compare freestanding and built-in baths. Look at the usable bathing space, access to taps, surrounding ledges and whether you can reach every surface for cleaning.

A back-to-wall bath may also be worth considering where you want a different appearance without leaving an awkward gap behind the tub.

The important question is whether the bath fits alongside everything else you need. [Reece's guidance on including a bath recommends assessing the space remaining after allowing for the shower, vanity, toilet and storage.][2]

## When replacing the bath with a shower makes sense

Removing an unused bath can create an opportunity to improve the room.

Depending on the layout, that space could accommodate a more generous shower, additional storage or a clearer path through the bathroom.

It may also allow you to reposition your bathroom vanity, giving drawers and cupboard doors enough room to open comfortably.

### You want a shower that is easier to enter

If stepping over the side of a bath is uncomfortable, discuss whether a walk-in shower could suit your needs.

Think beyond the screen. Consider the entrance width, floor level changes, slip resistance, tap placement and whether future support rails may be useful.

[The Australian Government's Your Home guidance recommends considering circulation space, slip-resistant surfaces, showers without hobs and wall reinforcement for future grab rails when designing adaptable wet areas.][3] These are planning considerations, not a guarantee that any particular shower meets an individual's accessibility needs.

### Your current bathroom feels crowded

Removing the bath is worth exploring when it prevents you from comfortably using the shower, toilet or vanity.

However, compare a revised layout before committing. Sometimes a different bath or vanity arrangement can solve the problem while preserving both bathing options.

See our guide to [fitting a bath and separate shower in a small bathroom](#recommended-reading) for the questions to consider.

## Compare the two options

| Your situation | Keeping a bath may suit you when… | Replacing it may suit you when… |
|---|---|---|
| **Daily routine** | Someone regularly uses and enjoys it. | Everyone showers and the bath sits unused. |
| **Available space** | Fixtures fit comfortably with useful storage. | The bath restricts movement or shower access. |
| **Changing needs** | Bathing remains comfortable and practical. | Stepping into the bath is becoming difficult. |
| **Other bathrooms** | This bath provides a useful option for the household. | Another bathroom already has a suitable bath. |
| **Renovation priorities** | Retaining bathing options matters most. | Shower comfort, storage or circulation matters more. |
| **Selling plans** | Local buyer feedback supports retaining it. | You have assessed buyer preferences and the proposed layout. |

Use this as a starting point. A measured floor plan will reveal more than a list of preferences alone.

## What a bath-to-shower conversion involves

A conversion needs a complete scope of work. Removing the bath is only one part of the job.

Ask your bathroom renovation builder to explain what is included in each stage.

### Removal and assessment

The bath, surrounding finishes and affected fittings are removed as required. The exposed walls and floor can then be assessed for damage and suitability for the proposed work.

Do not assume the surfaces beneath the bath are ready to become a shower floor. Your bathroom strip-out and preparation scope should explain what is being removed and how unexpected issues will be handled.

### Plumbing and drainage

The proposed shower needs suitable water supply and drainage arrangements. Ask whether existing connections can be used or whether bathroom plumbing and drainage need alteration.

In Queensland, regulated plumbing and drainage work requires an appropriately licensed tradesperson. [Ask the plumber to confirm the work category and any required paperwork, including Form 4 where applicable.][4]

### Waterproofing and surface preparation

The quote should explain how bathroom waterproofing will be addressed across the affected area.

[QBCC guidance emphasises suitable surface preparation, correct detailing, curing time and clear documentation of the waterproofing scope.][5] These stages should be allowed for in the programme before tiling proceeds.

### Tiles, shower screens and tapware

Confirm the extent of replacement bathroom floor and wall tiles. Ask whether matching existing tiles is realistic or whether a broader replacement is needed.

Discuss how your chosen shower screen will work with the entrance, nearby fittings and cleaning access.

Choose bathroom tapware with practical placement in mind. Consider whether you can reach the controls comfortably and where a handheld shower would sit.

### Lighting and finishing details

A changed layout is also a reason to review bathroom lighting, particularly around the vanity and mirror.

Discuss ventilation and exhaust fans as part of the renovation scope, along with towel rails, mirrors and other accessories.

Compare like-for-like quotes. A price covering removal and plumbing cannot be fairly compared with one that includes waterproofing, tiling, screening and completion.

## Cleaning and water use

### Which option is easier to clean?

Look at the specific design.

For a bath, consider whether you can reach the floor and wall behind it. For a shower, consider glass panels, tracks, grout joints, niches and access to the waste.

A recessed shower niche can provide a place for toiletries, but its position and surfaces should be considered alongside your cleaning routine.

Imagine cleaning the finished room with your usual equipment. If a gap is difficult to reach on the plan, it is worth resolving before installation.

### Will a shower use less water?

It depends on the shower's flow rate, how long it runs and how much water you would otherwise use in the bath.

[Australian Government guidance explains that shower flow rates appear on the water rating label in litres per minute.][6] Compare that figure when selecting a showerhead.

For illustration, a shower flowing at 9 litres per minute uses approximately 45 litres over five minutes, or 90 litres over ten minutes. These calculations exclude water used while waiting for it to warm up.

A larger shower space does not itself determine water consumption. The fittings and your habits do.

## What if it is your only bath?

Treat removing the only bath as a separate decision from removing a second bath.

If you expect to stay for years, give considerable weight to how the bathroom will serve your household. If you intend to sell soon, seek local advice before finalising the layout.

Ask agents familiar with your property type:

- Do buyers in this market commonly ask for a bath?
- Would a shower-only bathroom narrow the likely buyer pool?
- Is the current room's poor layout a more significant concern?
- Are comparable homes offering both a bath and separate shower?

Avoid assuming there is a universal resale penalty or guaranteed value increase. The layout needs to be considered in the context of the property and its likely buyers.

## Frequently asked questions

### Can I replace a bath with a walk-in shower?

Potentially, but the room needs assessment first. Floor construction, drainage, waterproofing and the proposed shower entrance all influence what is practical.

### Can the shower use the existing bath drain?

Do not assume so. Have a licensed plumber assess whether the existing drainage arrangement is suitable or requires modification.

### Should I keep a bath for young children?

Consider your family's actual routine. If the bath is regularly used, retaining it may be worthwhile. Compare that benefit against the space needed for the rest of the bathroom.

### Is a walk-in shower automatically accessible?

No. Accessibility depends on the complete design and the person using it. Entry, circulation space, surfaces, controls and support requirements all need consideration.

### How much does a bath-to-shower conversion cost?

The cost depends on the removal work, plumbing changes, waterproofing, tiling, screen and selected fittings. A site-specific bathroom renovation quote should identify inclusions, exclusions and allowances so you can compare options properly.
